\section{\partlabel{1}Prelab Calculations}

All four circuits charge through the same \qty{1}{\mega\ohm} from the same \qty{15}{\volt}, so the time constant is set by the capacitance alone: \qty{47}{\second}, \qty{100}{\second}, \qty{147}{\second} and \qty{32}{\second}. Every circuit reaches \qty{9.48}{\volt} after \ntau{1} charging and falls to \qty{5.52}{\volt} after \ntau{1} discharging, and the current starts at \qty{15}{\micro\ampere} in all four.

\begin{marginfigure}
  \input{plots/circuits}
  \caption{The four RC circuits from the Part~1 table, redrawn from the handout, with the AV probes from the Part~2 Multisim build. Each probe reads its node's voltage to ground and the current in the direction of its arrow, into the capacitor, and is named for its circuit's time constant.}
  \label{fig:circuits}
\end{marginfigure}

\subsection{Equivalent capacitance}

Capacitors in parallel add, so Circuit~3 is \qty{147}{\micro\farad}:
\begin{equation}
  \Ceq = \qty{47}{\micro\farad} + \qty{100}{\micro\farad} = \qty{147}{\micro\farad}
\end{equation}

Capacitors in series combine the way parallel resistors do, so Circuit~4 is smaller than either capacitor:
\begin{equation}
  \Ceq = \frac{\qty{47}{\micro\farad} \cdot \qty{100}{\micro\farad}}
              {\qty{47}{\micro\farad} + \qty{100}{\micro\farad}}
       = \qty{31.97}{\micro\farad} \approx \qty{32}{\micro\farad}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Time constants and expected values}

With $R = \qty{1}{\mega\ohm}$, each microfarad adds one second,\sidenote{$\qty{1}{\mega\ohm} \cdot \qty{1}{\micro\farad} = \qty{e6}{\ohm} \cdot \qty{e-6}{\farad} = \qty{1}{\second}$.} so $\tau$ in seconds equals the capacitance in \unit{\micro\farad}:
\begin{equation}
  \tau = R \cdot C = \qty{1}{\mega\ohm} \cdot \qty{47}{\micro\farad} = \qty{47}{\second}
\end{equation}

While charging, the capacitor voltage climbs toward \Vs{} and gets \qty{63.2}{\percent} of the way there in one time constant:
\begin{equation}
  v_C(t) = \Vs\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right) \qquad
  v_C(\tau) = 0.632 \cdot \qty{15}{\volt} = \qty{9.48}{\volt}
\end{equation}

While discharging, it falls from its starting voltage and has \qty{36.8}{\percent} left after one time constant:
\begin{equation}
  v_C(t) = V_0\,e^{-t/\tau} \qquad
  v_C(\tau) = 0.368 \cdot \qty{15}{\volt} = \qty{5.52}{\volt}
\end{equation}

At $t = 0$ all \qty{15}{\volt} sits across the \qty{1}{\mega\ohm}: when charging because the empty capacitor acts as a short, and when discharging because the full capacitor is the source. That is the maximum current, and it decays with the same $\tau$ either way:
\begin{gather}
  I_{C,\mathrm{MAX}} =\frac{\Vs}{R} = \frac{\qty{15}{\volt}}{\qty{1}{\mega\ohm}}
    = \qty{15}{\micro\ampere} \\
  i_C(\tau) = 0.368 \cdot \qty{15}{\micro\ampere} = \qty{5.52}{\micro\ampere}
\end{gather}

None of the voltages or currents depend on the capacitance; only the time it takes to reach them does (Table~\ref{tab:prelab}).

With the sources removed and every capacitor starting at \qty{15}{\volt}, each voltage falls to \qty{36.8}{\percent} at its own time constant. The readings mirror Part~2: every \ntau{1} voltage is \qty{15}{\volt} minus its charging value, and Circuit~3 again takes the longest and Circuit~4 the shortest.

\subsection{Multisim}

The Part~2 file was saved as a copy, the four sources were removed and replaced with a short (Figure~\ref{fig:discharge-circuits}). An initial condition on each capacitor takes the place of the charging step: \qty{15}{\volt} on Circuits 1--3, including both capacitors in Circuit~3, since parallel capacitors share the same voltage.

Circuit~4's two capacitors are in series, so each carries the full series charge \Qt{}, and the \qty{15}{\volt} splits between them in inverse proportion to capacitance:
\begin{gather}
  \Qt = \Ceq \cdot \Vs = \qty{31.97}{\micro\farad} \cdot \qty{15}{\volt} =\qty{0.480}{\milli\coulomb} \\
  V_{\qty{100}{\micro\farad}} = \frac{\qty{0.480}{\milli\coulomb}}{\qty{100}{\micro\farad}} = \qty{4.80}{\volt}
  \qquad
  V_{\qty{47}{\micro\farad}} = \frac{\qty{0.480}{\milli\coulomb}}{\qty{47}{\micro\farad}} = \qty{10.20}{\volt}
\end{gather}

The smaller series capacitor has a larger voltage drop, and the two add back to \qty{15}{\volt}. The analysis was re-run to \qty{1000}{\second}, plotting the four voltages (Figure~\ref{fig:discharge-v}) and then the four currents (Figure~\ref{fig:discharge-i}). The probes were left as in Part~2, so the current now flows out of the capacitor against the arrow and reads negative.

\subsection{Results}

Circuit~3 took the longest to discharge and Circuit~4 the shortest (Figure~\ref{fig:discharge-v}), the same order as charging and for the same reason. More capacitance holds more charge at \qty{15}{\volt}, and the same \qty{1}{\mega\ohm} drains it no faster than \qty{15}{\micro\ampere}.

The current curves rank the same way (Figure~\ref{fig:discharge-i}). With only the \qty{1}{\mega\ohm} resistor in the loop, the current is the capacitor voltage divided by \qty{1}{\mega\ohm},\sidenote{\qty{1}{\volt} across \qty{1}{\mega\ohm} is \qty{1}{\micro\ampere}.} so each current curve is its voltage curve flipped negative. That is why the \unit{\micro\ampere} readings in Table~\ref{tab:discharge} match the volt readings.

Each \ntau{1} reading is the Part~2 value turned over, $\qty{5.498}{\volt} = \qty{15}{\volt} - \qty{9.502}{\volt}$ for Circuit~1 and likewise for the rest. Charging and discharging follow the same exponential, one rising toward \qty{15}{\volt} and the other falling toward \qty{0}{\volt}. Circuit~4's \qty{+2.85}{\percent} error comes from the same \qty{20}{\second} point spacing as in Part~2. Its \ntau{5} reading of \qty{0.091}{\volt} is close to the expected \qty{0.10}{\volt}. On a value that small, the percent error says more about the measurement technique.

\section{\partlabel{4}Hardware Verification of RC Transient Responses}

\begin{marginfigure}
  \input{plots/meter}
  \caption{Step~5's measurement, redrawn from the handout with the DMM drawn as its internal resistance. \Rvm{} and R13 form a voltage divider, so the meter reads only the voltage across \Rvm, \Vm, rather than that of the full \qty{15}{\volt} supply.}
  \label{fig:meter}
\end{marginfigure}%
On the breadboard each capacitor still crossed the magic \qty{63.2}{\percent} figure close to its own \RC. The scope captures put six of the eight time constants within \qty{4.3}{\percent} under \RC, and the discharges of Circuits 1 and~4 \qty{5.9}{\percent} and \qty{3.3}{\percent} over it. Counting the meter's internal resistance, all of them should have come in \qty{9.4}{\percent} under, so the hardware ran slower than that prediction. The stopwatch times ran \qtyrange{0.3}{5.7}{\percent} long.

\subsection{Calculation prep}

On the DMM the supply read \qty{15.008}{\volt} straight from the \qty{+15}{\volt} rail to ground, and \qty{13.592}{\volt} through the \qty{1}{\mega\ohm} (Figure~\ref{fig:meter}). The second reading is lower because the DMM passes a small amount of current. Its internal resistance \Rvm{} draws \qty{1.42}{\micro\ampere} through the \qty{1}{\mega\ohm}, which drops \qty{1.42}{\volt}. The two resistors are a\pagebreak[3] voltage divider, so VDR solved for \Rvm{} gives the meter's resistance:\sidenote{\Rvm{} calculated to \qty{9.598}{\mega\ohm}, which is close to the handout's hint of about \qty{10}{\mega\ohm}.}
\begin{gather}
  \Vm = \Vs\,\frac{\Rvm}{\Rvm + R} \\
  \Rvm = R\,\frac{\Vm}{\Vs - \Vm}
       = \qty{1}{\mega\ohm} \cdot \frac{\qty{13.592}{\volt}}{\qty{15.008}{\volt} - \qty{13.592}{\volt}}
       = \qty{9.598}{\mega\ohm}
\end{gather}

The DMM stays across the capacitor for the rest of Part~4, so the same divider sets the highest voltage the capacitor can reach through the \qty{1}{\mega\ohm}:
\begin{equation}
  \Vmax = \Vs\,\frac{\Rvm}{\Rvm + \qty{1}{\mega\ohm}}
        = \qty{15.008}{\volt} \cdot \frac{\qty{9.598}{\mega\ohm}}{\qty{10.598}{\mega\ohm}}
        = \qty{13.59}{\volt}
\end{equation}
This matches the step~5 reading, as the handout predicts. After one time constant the capacitor should reach \qty{63.2}{\percent} of \Vmax{}, or \qty{8.59}{\volt}, so the stopwatch was stopped at that voltage.

\subsection{Charging}

\begin{figure*}
  \input{plots/bench}\par
  \vspace{15.5pt}% the same drawing-to-caption gap as Figure 1
  \caption{Part~4 circuits, redrawn from the handout (steps 8 and 14) with each DMM drawn as its internal resistance \Rvm{} (step 15). Circuits 2--4 put their capacitors in C13's and C14's place.}
  \label{fig:bench}
\end{figure*}

The charging circuit from handout step~8 was wired with the \qty{1}{\mega\ohm} on one throw of the switch, the \qty{47}{\ohm} on the other, and the DMM across the capacitor (Figure~\ref{fig:bench}, left). In one position the capacitor charges through the \qty{1}{\mega\ohm}; in the other the \qty{47}{\ohm} empties it for the next run, with a time constant of only \qty{2.2}{\milli\second}. For each circuit the switch was flipped as the stopwatch started, and the stopwatch was stopped when the DMM read \qty{8.59}{\volt}. Each circuit was timed once (Table~\ref{tab:charge-hw}).

Every charge and discharge was also recorded on a PicoScope, on its \qty{\pm 50}{\volt} range at \qty{50}{\second}/div, or \qty{100}{\second}/div for Circuit~3, whose \ntau{5} of \qty{735}{\second} would not fit on a \qty{500}{\second} screen. Instead of reading the scope's cursors, the CSV exports were analyzed: an exponential fitted to each capture gives the moment the switch flipped, the voltage the capacitor was heading for, and $\tau$ (Figure~\ref{fig:scope}).\sidenote[-36pt]{With the capacitor shorted through the \qty{47}{\ohm} the scope read \qty{0.41}{\volt}, so every trace is shifted down by that much. The \qty{\pm 50}{\volt} range moves in \qty{0.46}{\volt} steps; averaging the samples in each time bin smooths them out.}

For the captures the DMM was removed and the scope was connected across the capacitor through a \texttimes 10 probe, so the scope runs and the stopwatch runs were separate runs. \subsection{Discharging}

For the discharge runs the \qty{47}{\ohm} and \qty{1}{\mega\ohm} were swapped (Figure~\ref{fig:bench}, right). Now the capacitor charges through the \qty{47}{\ohm} within milliseconds, and it discharges through the \qty{1}{\mega\ohm} slowly enough to time. In the discharge position the DMM's \Rvm{} sits across the capacitor in parallel with the \qty{1}{\mega\ohm}, so the capacitor discharges through
\begin{equation}
  \Req = \qty{1}{\mega\ohm} \parallel \Rvm
       = \frac{\qty{1}{\mega\ohm} \cdot \qty{9.598}{\mega\ohm}}{\qty{1}{\mega\ohm} + \qty{9.598}{\mega\ohm}}
       = \qty{905.6}{\kilo\ohm}
\end{equation}
and every time constant is \qty{9.4}{\percent} shorter than \RC:
\begin{equation}
  \tau_{\mathrm{adj}} = \Req \cdot C = \qty{905.6}{\kilo\ohm} \cdot \qty{47}{\micro\farad} =\qty{42.57}{\second}
\end{equation}

The meter belongs in the charging prediction as much as the discharging one. While charging, the capacitor sees the \qty{1}{\mega\ohm} and \Rvm{} as a Thevenin resistance of the same \qty{905.6}{\kilo\ohm}, charging toward \Vmax{} instead of \qty{15}{\volt}. So both tables should be held to the adjusted $\tau$, and every capture came out slower than it, by \qtyrange{5.6}{10.2}{\percent} (\qty{14.1}{\percent} for Circuit~4's discharge and \qty{17}{\percent} for Circuit~1's).\sidenote{During the captures the probe's \qty{10}{\mega\ohm} stood in for \Rvm, making the resistance \qty{909}{\kilo\ohm} instead of \qty{905.6}{\kilo\ohm}. Against that the captures run \qtyrange{5.2}{9.8}{\percent} slow, and \qty{13.7}{\percent} and \qty{16.5}{\percent} for the discharges of Circuits 4 and~1.} Since \Rvm{} was measured, the rest is most likely the parts themselves: electrolytic capacitors are usually rated \qty{\pm 20}{\percent}, and capacitance 6 to \qty{10}{\percent} over the marked value would cover all but the discharges of Circuits 1 and~4. The \qty{1}{\mega\ohm}'s \qty{\pm 5}{\percent} could account for some of it too. Neither part was measured on its own, so the two can't be separated.

\section*{Conclusion}

\begin{marginfigure}
  \input{plots/ceq}
  \caption{\Ceq{} formulas for capacitors in both series and parallel. Both formulas (except for the series capacitance shortcut shown) are associative and commutative, and can therefore be repeated for an arbitrary number of elements.}
  \label{fig:ceq}
\end{marginfigure}

\begin{question}
  State which charges faster, a small capacitor or a large capacitor. Describe the effect of putting capacitors in parallel or series on the charging rate and total capacitance in the circuit.
\end{question}
\begin{answer}
  The small capacitor charges faster. Through the same resistor $\tau = R \cdot C$ grows with $C$, so the \qty{32}{\micro\farad} series pair reached \qty{63.2}{\percent} in \qty{31.9}{\second} on the scope and the \qty{147}{\micro\farad} parallel pair took \qty{143.8}{\second}. Capacitors in parallel add, so the combination is larger than either one and charges more slowly. Capacitors in series combine the way parallel resistors do, so the combination is smaller than either one and charges faster. The time constants measured on the bench combine the same way: \qty{45.0}{\second} + \qty{96.3}{\second} = \qty{141.3}{\second} against \qty{143.8}{\second} measured in parallel, and the series formula gives \qty{30.7}{\second} against \qty{31.9}{\second} measured.
\end{answer}

\begin{question}
  What effect does the time constant have on either charging or discharging? What effect would changing the value of resistance have on the time constant?
\end{question}
\begin{answer}
  The time constant scales the capacitor's rate of approach to \qty{100}{\percent} charge. After \ntau{1} it is \qty{63.2}{\percent} charged or has \qty{36.8}{\percent} left, whatever $\tau$ is, and after \ntau{5} it is within \qty{0.7}{\percent} of the end value. A larger $\tau$ stretches the same curve out in time, which is why all eight captures look alike in Figure~\ref{fig:scope} once time is divided by \RC. Since $\tau = R \cdot C$, $\tau$ is proportional to $R$: doubling the resistance doubles it.
\end{answer}

\section*{Errors and Difficulties}

Each circuit was timed once with the stopwatch, so there is no spread to show how repeatable the times are, and the scope captures were also one run each. Multiple trials would generally shed more certainty on the experiment.

The \qty{1}{\mega\ohm} charge/discharge resistor suits the stopwatch procedure, which needs time constants long enough to time by hand, but the scope procedure actually used could have employed a much smaller value. With \qty{10}{\kilo\ohm}, every $\tau$ would be under \qty{1.5}{\second}, a full charge and discharge of Circuit~3 would take about \qty{15}{\second} instead of \qty{25}{\minute}, and many more trials would fit in the same lab time.

The scope's \qty{\pm 50}{\volt} range moves in \qty{0.46}{\volt} steps, about \qty{3}{\percent} of the signal, and it read \qty{0.41}{\volt} with the capacitor shorted. Averaging and the offset correction take care of both for $\tau$, which depends only on the curve's shape, but absolute voltages read from the scope, like the final voltages in Table~\ref{tab:charge-hw}, are only good to a couple of tenths of a volt. A smaller range would have given finer steps. The scope was also not triggered by the switch, so the flip time had to be found from each trace.
